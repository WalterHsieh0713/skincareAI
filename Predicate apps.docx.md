Dewpoint — 5 Predicate Apps

Competitive & strategic model analysis produced by Ivy (Marketing Strategist).

# 1. Cal AI — AI Food Logger / Calorie Counter

**What it does:** Users photograph a meal; Cal AI returns an instant calorie count and macro breakdown with no manual logging. The photo is the entire input. A daily streak mechanic drives compulsive return behavior, and the app has built a large organic TikTok presence through user-posted “scan this” content.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Cal AI is Dewpoint’s explicit structural template. Photo → objective number → trend/streak maps 1:1. Both monetize the anxiety of “am I making progress?” through a repeatable daily ritual that produces a feedback signal the user couldn’t generate themselves.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** The score reveal is a product moment, not just a UI transition. Cal AI makes the number feel like the punchline — you take the photo, you wait one beat, you get the hit. Dewpoint’s skin score reveal needs that same dopaminergic design: large animated number, trend arrow, delta from yesterday. Don’t bury it. Also steal the streak integer — not a progress bar, not a chart, just “Day 14.” That number is doing enormous retention work on its own.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** Cal AI’s moat is shallow. The photo-to-number mechanic is a commodity — every major food logger is adding a camera. Dewpoint avoids this trap by making the guided capture the moat, not the score itself. An un-normalized score is noise; the coaching that makes scores comparable is the defensible layer. Dewpoint should communicate this explicitly in onboarding rather than leading with “AI skin score” — because the AI skin score is the commodity, and the normalization is the product.

# 2. Yuka — Food and Beauty Ingredient Scanner

**What it does:** Users scan a product barcode; Yuka returns a color-coded safety grade (A through D) based on ingredient composition — for both food and cosmetics. Its founding brand promise is strict commercial independence: no brand sponsorships, no affiliate revenue, no paid placements. That promise is the product.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Direct overlap with Feature 4. Same core mechanic — scan, parse INCI, surface what matters. Same trust positioning — brand neutrality as the differentiator from brand-owned tools, which structurally cannot be neutral. Yuka proved this positioning scales to tens of millions of users and $30M+ in revenue on a subscription model.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** Yuka doesn’t make you read an ingredient list. It collapses the complexity into a letter grade and surfaces the two or three things that actually matter. Dewpoint’s conflict checker should follow this same compression: don’t return a wall of INCI — return “2 active conflicts, 1 flagged irritant for your concern profile” and let the user drill in. Also steal the content format: “what’s actually in your [product]” is a proven TikTok mechanic with high shareability.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** Yuka’s grades are binary — a “bad” ingredient for one person is completely benign for another with different concerns and barrier function. This has generated ongoing brand pushback and user confusion. Dewpoint’s ingredient audit should be explicitly personalized to the user’s stated concerns, not a universal clean/dirty verdict. That’s not just a feature improvement — it’s the strategic differentiator from Yuka in the beauty vertical.

# 3. Flo Health — Women’s Cycle and Health Tracker

**What it does:** Flo tracks menstrual cycles, ovulation windows, and daily symptoms, delivering personalized AI insights and health content. It has evolved from a simple period tracker into a full health platform with $200M+ ARR and one of the highest subscription conversion rates in consumer health — estimated at 15–20% of monthly actives.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Flo’s core user is anxious about a biological process she cannot directly observe, making small daily inputs to generate a feedback signal that answers the recurring question: “is this normal?” That loop — invisible process, daily log, anxiety-to-insight translation — is structurally identical to Dewpoint’s “is my skin actually getting better?” loop. Flo is the proof of concept for subscription monetization of health anxiety with no hardware.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** The insight card format. Flo doesn’t show you a raw chart — it delivers a narrative. “Your luteal phase is running 2 days longer this cycle than your average.” Dewpoint should build this pattern into the score screen: not a graph of numbers, a sentence with meaning. “Your redness score has been trending down for 11 days — the longest streak you’ve had since starting your retinol.” Also steal Flo’s paywall placement: the log is free, the interpretation costs money.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** In 2021 the FTC found Flo had shared health data with Facebook and Google after explicitly promising not to. In a post-Flo environment, any app collecting daily health-adjacent photo data is under a microscope — and skin condition data is arguably more sensitive because it contains facial biometrics. Dewpoint should be aggressively, visibly privacy-first from day one: on-device processing wherever possible, explicit no-third-party-sharing policies surfaced in onboarding, not buried in a terms page.

# 4. Skinive — AI Dermatology Screening Tool

**What it does:** Skinive analyzes photos of skin lesions and surface conditions using computer vision, flagging potential dermatological concerns (melanoma risk, eczema, psoriasis) and recommending dermatologist consultation. It has pursued medical device registration in multiple markets and positions as a clinical screening aid.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Highest technical overlap of any app in this list. Photo-based skin analysis, guided capture, multi-condition detection. For Features 1 and 2, Skinive is the most direct technical predecessor — they’ve solved many of the same capture normalization and image analysis problems Dewpoint needs to solve.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** Skinive’s capture coaching UX is more mature than most consumer apps because their use case — lesion detection — tolerates zero ambiguity in photo conditions. Their distance calibration, lighting prompts, and positioning guides are worth studying closely as a UX reference for Dewpoint’s guided scan flow. Borrow that rigor.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** Skinive pursued clinical positioning and got trapped by it. The moment an app positions as a diagnostic tool, it inherits a 3–5 year regulatory approval cycle and a user expectation it structurally cannot meet. Dewpoint’s explicitly non-diagnostic framing — “image-derived indicators, not clinical or diagnostic measurements” — is strategically correct. That language should appear not just in the Terms but in onboarding, on every result screen, and in every marketing asset.

# 5. Think Dirty — Cosmetic Ingredient Safety Scanner

**What it does:** Think Dirty lets users scan beauty product barcodes and returns a “Dirty Meter” score (0–10) rating ingredient safety, with citations from toxicology databases. It also surfaces “clean” product alternatives — which is how it monetizes, through a built-in shop with affiliate and brand-partnership revenue.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Direct overlap with Feature 4. Same mechanic — barcode scan, ingredient parse, concern flags. Think Dirty pioneered this format in beauty and built a large catalog and loyal user base before Yuka entered the US market.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** The “bathroom cabinet” feature — users can catalog their full product collection and get a portfolio-level view of their ingredient exposure over time. That mechanic is significantly stickier than one-off scans because it creates an ongoing data asset the user doesn’t want to lose. Dewpoint should build the parallel: log the products in your routine, and surface a stack-level conflict report — “between your AM and PM routine, you have three active ingredient pairings that may be counterproductive.” That’s a weekly retention touchpoint, not a one-time feature.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** Think Dirty collapsed its own trust positioning. The app scores a product as “dirty” and then surfaces a paid “clean” alternative in the same screen. Once users understood that the recommendation was a revenue stream, the brand-neutral credibility evaporated. This is the single most important trap for Dewpoint’s ingredient feature. The moment the conflict checker surfaces affiliate product recommendations, the moat drains. Keep Feature 4 commercially sterile — no brand partnerships, no affiliate revenue, no product recommendations in the ingredient flow. The subscription model funds it. The trust it buys is worth more than the affiliate margin would be.

# The Through-Line

The apps that built durable businesses (Cal AI’s growth, Yuka’s trust, Flo’s retention) all did one thing structurally — they made the user feel like the product was working for them, not selling to them. The apps that stalled or got caught (Think Dirty’s trust collapse, Skinive’s regulatory trap) drifted from their founding positioning under monetization pressure or ambition creep.

Dewpoint’s defensible position is specific: objective self-comparison, not beauty ideals; brand-neutral education, not product sales; image-derived indicators, not clinical claims. That frame should survive every monetization decision intact.

Part 2: Derma / Cosmetic / Beauty Apps

Five additional predicate apps specifically from the dermatology, cosmetics, and beauty vertical.

# 6. Neutrogena Skin360 — Skin Analysis + Hardware Peripheral

**What it does:** Johnson & Johnson's Skin360 paired a smartphone app with a physical SkinScanner hardware attachment (a clip-on macro lens) to capture high-magnification selfies, then scored users on pores, texture, fine lines, and moisture. It produced a personalized Skin360 Score and recommended Neutrogena products. The app was discontinued in 2023.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Both center on a repeatable selfie-based scoring loop that tracks a normalized skin-condition score over time. Both attempt to make invisible, slow-moving skin change legible through numbers.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** The score-over-time chart was genuinely compelling — users reported checking it the way they check step counts. The specific mechanic worth stealing: surface the delta, not just the score. "Your texture improved 8 points in 3 weeks" lands harder than showing a number in isolation. Build the trend comparison into the score reveal screen.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** Hardware killed them. Requiring a $30 clip-on peripheral created a purchase barrier that collapsed the funnel before users ever got their first score. Dewpoint's software-only normalized capture is a structural advantage — never reintroduce friction by making the scan feel like it requires special equipment. Make the ordinary front camera feel like the right tool.

# 7. Curology — Telehealth Prescription Skincare

**What it does:** Curology connects users with licensed dermatology providers via an async photo-consultation flow: submit intake photos of your skin concerns, get a custom prescription formula (tretinoin, niacinamide, azelaic acid, etc.) formulated by a provider, reorder monthly. Users submit check-in photos periodically and providers adjust formulations. The model monetizes at ~$30–40/month.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Both are built on a photo-submission loop tied to an ongoing skincare routine. Both ask users to invest in a recurring, monthly relationship with their own skin data. Curology's retention engine is the custom formula; Dewpoint's is the adherence tracker.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** Curology's intake flow is masterful. Before you spend a dollar, you've already told them your skin story — concerns, history, product reactions, goals. That self-disclosure creates commitment and perceived personalization even before the product ships. Dewpoint should implement a skin onboarding interview (not a generic quiz) that asks users to articulate their specific concern and what they've already tried. It converts skeptics into invested users before the first scan.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** Curology's authority derives entirely from the human provider — which means it can never scale without adding headcount, and it creates a liability dependency on clinical judgment. Dewpoint's brand-neutral, self-comparison framing is the right call. Never position the score as clinical or imply a provider is behind it, or you inherit all of Curology's regulatory overhead without any of its actual prescription moat.

# 8. SkinVision — AI Skin Lesion Risk Detection

**What it does:** SkinVision uses computer vision to analyze photos of moles, spots, and lesions for skin cancer risk indicators (melanoma, basal cell carcinoma). Users photograph a lesion, get a low/medium/high risk assessment, and are directed to seek dermatologist care if flagged. It operates in Europe, Australia, and the US with CE/FDA regulatory clearances.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Both are AI-powered photo-analysis apps that produce a scored output from a skin image. Both require users to submit close-up skin photos in a repeatable way. Both sit in the space between "not a medical device" and "useful health signal."

**What Dewpoint should steal:** SkinVision's disclaimer architecture is elegant — they position the output as a "risk check" that informs a conversation with a doctor, not a diagnosis. Dewpoint needs equivalently tight language: the score is a "relative skin condition indicator tracked against your own baseline" — not a measurement, not a diagnosis, not clinical. Build that disclaimer language into the score reveal screen itself, not buried in ToS.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** SkinVision's single-use case is its ceiling. Users open it when they're worried about a spot, get a result, then never open it again. There's no habit loop because there's nothing to track week-over-week in a meaningful way. Dewpoint's multi-axis + trend + routine loop is the right architecture precisely because it creates a reason to return every day. Never let the scan feel like a one-time verdict.

# 9. L'Oréal Skin Genius — Brand-Owned AI Skin Diagnosis

**What it does:** Skin Genius is L'Oréal's AI-powered facial analysis tool embedded directly in the L'Oréal e-commerce site. Users take a selfie, receive a skin-type and skin-concern profile (oily, dry, sensitive, hyperpigmentation, etc.), and are immediately served a curated product recommendation list — all L'Oréal products. Deployed across L'Oréal's brand portfolio (Lancôme, Garnier, CeraVe).

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Both analyze a selfie to produce a skin-condition profile. Both map concerns (redness, texture, blemishes) from the image. The underlying computer vision task is similar.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** Skin Genius's session initiation rate is reportedly very high because the entry point is zero-commitment — the scan is a 10-second detour that makes your cart feel smarter. Dewpoint should borrow this energy for its first scan: make the first scan feel like something you can finish in 30 seconds, not a setup process. The scan is the hook, not a feature you unlock after onboarding.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** This is the exact conflict of interest that is Dewpoint's trust moat — and worth naming explicitly. Skin Genius cannot be trusted by users to give an unbiased read because the output routes directly to a purchase. Dewpoint should actively market against this positioning: "We don't sell products. We don't have a brand deal. The score is yours." That differentiation should be in onboarding copy, App Store description, and TikTok hooks. The trust gap these tools created is a gift — do not give it back by partnering with a brand that gets to see scan data.

# 10. Atolla — Subscription Serum with Iterative Skin Tracking

**What it does:** Atolla builds a personalized skincare serum based on an intake quiz covering concerns, environment, lifestyle, and existing routine. Users receive a monthly serum, complete a brief photo check-in and re-survey, and Atolla adjusts the formula for the next shipment. The feedback loop is the product — the serum is supposed to get better at treating your skin over time as the model learns. Acquired by Unilever in 2021.

**Overlap with Dewpoint:** Atolla runs the closest structural analog to Dewpoint's core loop: photo check-in + logged routine + tracked outcome = iterative improvement signal. Both treat the user's ongoing skin data as the asset. Both are subscription models where retention is earned by making users feel like they're getting a personalized, improving result.

**What Dewpoint should steal:** Atolla's "your formula is improving" framing is psychologically powerful — it turns the subscription into an investment that compounds rather than a cost that repeats. Dewpoint should borrow this language for the routine + scan combination: "Your data is building a clearer picture of what works for your skin." The longer you use it, the more valuable the trend data. Frame tenure as an asset, not just elapsed time.

**What Dewpoint should avoid:** Atolla's check-in rate collapsed because the survey felt like homework and users couldn't see a clear connection between their input and their formula change — the feedback loop was opaque. Dewpoint must close this loop visibly: when a scan shows improvement, surface it next to the routine adherence data from that same window. "Your skin texture improved 11 points during a stretch where you hit 87% routine adherence" makes the feedback loop felt, not abstract.
